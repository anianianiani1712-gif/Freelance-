What is the hedonic treadmill?
Why do the things we want stop making us happy so quickly?

Whether it's a dream holiday, a career milestone, or a personal goal, we often convince ourselves that reaching a specific destination will permanently alter our sense of wellbeing. While achieving these goals brings an initial rush of excitement, the feeling rarely lasts. Before long, we find ourselves searching for the next source of gratification.

This mechanism lies at the heart of hedonism — the philosophical idea that pleasure is the ultimate pursuit of human life. However, this desire is inherently insatiable. Psychologists refer to this phenomenon as the "hedonic treadmill": our tendency to rapidly adapt to life's highs and lows, eventually returning to a relatively stable baseline of happiness.

The happiness reset

A landmark 1978 study published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology highlighted this effect by tracking lottery winners, accident victims with paralysis, and a control group. The findings revealed that lottery winners experienced an immediate surge in happiness, which reverted to baseline levels within a year. Conversely, while accident victims suffered intense initial distress, their happiness levels largely stabilised over time, ending up only slightly lower than those of the lottery winners.

According to hedonic adaptation theory, major life events — whether positive or negative — have short-lived effects on our overall happiness. However, subsequent research indicates that adaptation is not universal: chronic stressors such as grief, poverty, or prolonged social isolation can exert longer-lasting damage on wellbeing.

Beyond the next big thing

While the idea of hedonic adaptation may seem demotivating, integrative psychotherapist Dr Victoria Froome emphasizes that it can actually be empowering. Understanding that external milestones offer temporary satisfaction shifts the focus inward, highlighting that genuine contentment is an internal endeavor.

"The events that yield lasting benefits are those that reinforce your core identity, relationships, resilience, values, and strengths," explains Dr Froome. Chasing external targets endlessly often leads to burnout. By defining personal values and aligning life choices with them, individuals build a grounded sense of self that does not rely on constant external validation.

Less chasing, more living

Stepping off the treadmill requires re-evaluating how we define happiness. Therapeutic modalities — such as Person-Centred Therapy, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), and Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) — can help individuals strengthen self-worth and reshape unhelpful thinking patterns, provided they are applied with self-compassion and proper pacing.

Additionally, mindfulness allows us to observe emotions without immediate reaction, fostering six essential foundations of long-term wellbeing: values, strengths, relationships, resilience, skills, and identity. Unlike fleeting external achievements, these internal structures remain stable as happiness fluctuates, serving as the true key to sustainable fulfilment.
